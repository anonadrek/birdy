Under each video: the beats (tap one to jump there), a scrubber, then the notes: why it lands, what it takes to make one a day automatically, effort, risks and how it ties back to Birdy.

Beats

    Why it lands

    You hear a bird and watch the sound take shape, which almost nobody has seen. The five-times-over pattern is visible before the answer, so viewers feel they worked it out. Sound-on platforms first (TikTok, Reels); the finished drawing doubles as a striking cover.

    Made daily by

    Inputs per species: one allowed recording, the hero photo with a focus point, the name and one checked fact. The spectrogram is a mask image made in Python (scipy, about 40 lines, as on this page). Each prototype here is already a pure function of time, so a Playwright script can step it at 30 fps, screenshot the stage at 1080 × 1920 and hand the frames and the clip to ffmpeg. Remotion would also work.

    Effort
    MAbout 3 days for the renderer and the recording picker, then automatic. The same renderer serves 2, 3 and 5.
    Risks

    Recording licences are the bottleneck. A first pass over Commons (each scientific name, audio files only) found a CC0, public domain or CC BY recording for about 45 of the 180 published species; 116 had only CC BY-SA recordings, mostly xeno-canto uploads, and 19 had none. It undercounts (this page's Song Thrush file is called "Taltrast.ogg" and was missed), but plan on this format for roughly one species in four, unless we ask recordists for permission or record our own. Species without one get concept 2 that day.

    No music anywhere: the bird is the soundtrack, so there is no music-rights question. Noisy recordings draw muddy shapes, so each clip gets a quick look (a column in the exceptions sheet).

    Ties to Birdy

    It is Birdy's sound ID made visible. In production add one line, "Birdy hears: Song Thrush", with the score from the V4 check, which already runs Birdy's own audio model on the chosen clip. Left out here because this clip has not been run.

    4:5 feed

    The frame where the bird shows faintly behind its finished song, question on top, as a still; the answer on carousel slide 2. Or the whole clip as a 4:5 feed video with a shorter spectrogram panel.

    Beats

      Why it lands

      It looks like a naturalist sketching live, and it teaches you to recognise the bird in fifteen seconds. The last frame is a finished journal page people save and come back to. Instagram and Facebook first (saves and shares), TikTok second.

      Made daily by

      The field marks come from the checked facts, but their positions on the photo do not. Each photo needs three points and a label side. Proposal: a vision model suggests the points from the fact list, the exceptions sheet shows a still with the arrows drawn, and Albin approves or nudges (about 20 seconds per species, once). Month bars from Artportalen via GBIF, the data the species pages already use; size from the facts. Same renderer as concept 1.

      Effort
      MShared renderer plus the point-approval step in the sheet. The approval is the slow part, not the code.
      Risks

      A misplaced arrow is a visible mistake on an ID account, so this format never publishes without approved points. Drawing on a photo is an adaptation: CC0, public domain and CC BY heroes only, which is 155 of the 180 published species. The 25 CC BY-SA heroes can still be shown uncropped with the notes beside them, not on them.

      Ties to Birdy

      The app's Field Journal look and the website's torn paper, in motion. It ends on "your journal keeps it", which is the app's diary.

      4:5 feed

      The final frame is the 4:5 feed post: plate on top, notes below. A carousel can step through the arrows one slide at a time.

      Beats

        Why it lands

        It is a game with a timer built in. People type their guess before the reveal, and comments are what Reels and TikTok reward most. It is also the post people send to the friend who "knows birds". Weekly, so it stays an event.

        Made daily by

        The silhouette is a mask of the real photo, never a drawn or generated bird. Here it is a brightness threshold plus clean-up in numpy and scipy (about 30 lines); busy backgrounds need a segmentation model, still only cutting out the real photo. Clues from the checked facts (habitat, one field mark), the call from the recording; without an allowed recording, clue 3 becomes a second field mark. Pick species with a shape you can guess: a small brown warbler in silhouette is not a fair question.

        Effort
        MTwo days on top of the shared renderer; every silhouette gets a quick look before it goes out.
        Risks

        Bad masks show at once (a stray branch reads as a tail). A silhouette is an adaptation, so CC0, public domain or CC BY photos only. Too easy or too hard kills the comments: keep a curated list of about 50 good shapes.

        Ties to Birdy

        Shape and sound are exactly Birdy's two ways in, photo ID and sound ID. The end card asks how many clues you needed, which is the app's promise in reverse.

        4:5 feed

        A carousel: silhouette, clue, clue, the photo. Swiping is the reveal, which suits Instagram and Facebook feeds.

        Mock-up. 1 is the oldest post, 12 the newest; Instagram puts the newest top left. Rows, newest first: a grebe spread, three Reel covers, a Great Tit spread, three single daily posts.

        1. a

          One horizon on every tile

          Every template puts its torn line at the same height, 74 % down the 3:4 grid crop, with the name below it. Any three neighbours then line up whatever column they land in, so one post a day can never break the look. The tear starts and ends at the same height, so the edges meet from tile to tile.

        2. b

          Spreads go up as a set of three

          A split image only reads as one picture if its three tiles land in one row. Post them within minutes, right tile first, then the middle, then the left: Instagram fills from the top left, so the newest post is always top left. Posted one a day instead, the rows below would sit one column off two days out of three.

        3. c

          Keep the count in threes

          Any extra grid post (an announcement, a repost) shifts every spread below by one tile. Put one-offs in Stories, or post them in threes too. Pinned posts sit at the start of the grid, so pin three or none.

        4. d

          Reel covers obey the same rule

          The grid shows a Reel as a centred 3:4 crop of its 9:16 cover. Every cover keeps its title and horizon inside that middle 3:4, so Reels sit in the grid like the photo posts. TikTok's profile grid crops covers in a similar way.

        5. e

          What it takes

          SThe horizon is a template rule, free once the renderers follow it. A spread is one wide render cut into three 4:5 files (half a day the first time). Risk: Instagram has changed its grid crop before (square to 3:4 in 2025), so the horizon height is a setting, not a constant.

        Beats

          Why it lands

          It teaches something real in fifteen seconds, the kind of post people save and send ("so that is how you tell them apart"). Two sound pictures on one time axis look like a page from a field guide, which is the brand.

          Made daily by

          Pairs come from the comparison pages (about 30 planned); the renderer is concept 1 with two clips on a shared axis and two photos.

          Effort
          SAbout a day once concept 1 exists.
          Risks

          Needs an allowed recording for both species, and only about 30 pairs exist, so every other Sunday rather than daily. Some pairs differ by sight rather than sound; those get the field-journal treatment instead.

          Ties to Birdy

          Each one links to its comparison page (the pages built to bring in search traffic), and "how do I tell them apart?" is answered by sound ID.

          4:5 feed

          Three-slide carousel: both photos ("same bird?"), both sound pictures, the names. The video is better here, since the point is the sound.

          Recommendation

          Launch with 1 and 2 as one daily slot. Each newly published species page gets a Reel the same day: "See the song" when an allowed recording exists, otherwise "The field journal comes alive". The schedule never has a gap, and the licence decides the format, not a person. With today's recordings that is mostly journals, so the cheapest way to more wow is more recordings: ask the xeno-canto recordists of our best clips for permission, or record a few species ourselves.

          Make the grid a rule from day one. Every cover follows the shared horizon, so the profile reads as one journal without extra work. Save true spreads for launch day and milestones (100 species), posted as a set of three.

          Add the two weekly events: "Who is it?" on Fridays (the day's species as a quiz) and "Same bird?" every other Sunday when the day's species has a comparison page. Both still count as that day's one post.

          Next steps

          1. Pick one recording per species and settle the real licence count (the first pass above is a lower bound).
          2. Turn this page's stages into the renderer: Playwright steps the time, ffmpeg adds the clip.
          3. Add three columns to the exceptions sheet: field-mark points, silhouette OK, recording OK.
          4. Render two weeks of posts before the accounts go live, then queue one a day.

          Posting: Meta Business Suite schedules Instagram and Facebook. TikTok's posting API keeps posts private until the app passes TikTok's review, so plan on uploading TikTok by hand at first (about two minutes a day).
